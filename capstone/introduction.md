
# Introduction

The statistical analysis of complex, evolving systems—ranging from global climate patterns and public health surveillance to financial market fluctuations—is increasingly forced to move beyond the traditional "stationary baseline" assumption. In many real-world applications, the underlying parameters of a process, such as its mean, trend, or dependence structure, do not remain constant but instead undergo abrupt shifts known as **changepoints**. Failing to account for these shifts can lead to biased trend estimates, inaccurate forecasts, and an inflation of false alarms in process monitoring. This paper explores the unified methodologies for detecting these structural breaks and modeling the resulting regimes, with a focus on robustness against common pitfalls like autocorrelation and parameter estimation uncertainty.

In climatology, the detection of regime shifts is essential for understanding the trajectory of global change. A prominent example is the investigation of whether a "warming surge" or acceleration in the global warming rate has occurred in the post-1970s era (Beaulieu et al., 2024). This study highlights a critical methodological constraint: although individual record-breaking years may suggest a shift, statistical significance depends on distinguishing a true structural change from internal variability and short-term noise. They demonstrate that a surge would currently require an increase of at least 55% in the existing warming trend to be detectable with 95% confidence. Similarly, the reorganization of global **tropical cyclone teleconnections** has been identified through a two-stage framework that combines changepoint detection with copula modeling (Hossain and Diawara, 2026). This research found a significant structural break in the year 2000, marking a 59% increase in North Atlantic storm frequency and a shift toward more complex, extreme-value-focused dependencies between ocean basins.

The theoretical foundation for identifying such shifts rests on **At-Most-One-Changepoint (AMOC)** procedures, which test the null hypothesis of homogeneity against an alternative of a single shift at an unknown time (Lund and Shi, 2026). Staple methods include the Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) and Likelihood Ratio Tests (LRT), though research indicates that the **Sum of Squared CUSUM (SCUSUM)** often provides superior detection power for mean shifts. However, a recurring danger in this field is the impact of **autocorrelation**. (Lund et al., 2023) emphasize that if positive serial dependence is ignored, standard changepoint tests frequently mistake long-term persistence for a structural shift, leading to spurious declarations of change. To mitigate this, practitioners are advised to "prewhiten" data—fitting an autoregressive (AR) model under the null hypothesis and applying tests to the resulting one-step-ahead prediction residuals.

Beyond detection, the monitoring of adverse outcomes in fields like medicine and public health relies on **Statistical Process Control (SPC)**. (Morton et al., 2002) illustrate how CUSUM and Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (EWMA) charts can alert clinicians to shifts in hospital-acquired infection rates while filtering out random fluctuations. A significant challenge in this context is the "plug-in" problem, where in-control parameters are estimated from a limited historical Phase I sample (Heidema et al., 2026). For small Poisson counts, such as rare disease clusters, **estimation uncertainty** can cause the false-alarm rate to vary wildly. To address this, a novel "exact approach" using Garwood limits has been proposed to ensure a **Guaranteed In-Control Performance (GICP)**, providing a mathematically rigorous way to set control limits that remain valid regardless of estimation error.

Furthermore, modeling the "tails" of distributions is crucial for assessing the risk of rare but catastrophic events. Traditional **Extreme Value Theory (EVT)** often involves choosing a threshold empirically, which discards data below the threshold and increases estimation variance (Behrens et al., 2004). (Behrens et al., 2004) propose a Bayesian mixture model that treats the threshold as an unknown parameter, combining a parametric center (e.g., Gamma) with a Generalized Pareto Distribution (GPD) for the tail. This approach provides more conservative and realistic risk estimates for heavy-tailed data, such as financial returns in the Nasdaq 100. For persistent or mean-reverting count time series that do not fit standard GPD or Poisson models, dynamic specifications such as the **Poisson Exponentially Weighted Moving Average (PEWMA)** or **Poisson Autoregressive (PAR)** models have been developed to capture evolving intensities over time (Brandt and Williams, 2000).

Despite these advancements, several **open problems** remain in the literature:

1.  **High-Dimensional Dependence Coherence:** Most current copula-based frameworks for non-stationary counts are limited to pairwise analysis. Extending these to higher dimensions ($d \geq 3$) requires implementing **vine copula constructions** to ensure that the complex inter-basin dependencies remain mathematically compatible across different regimes.
2.  **Autocorrelation in Discrete Non-Gaussian Processes:** While prewhitening is well-understood for Gaussian series, its application to discrete count processes (e.g., Poisson or Negative Binomial) is less mature. There is a need for unified frameworks that can simultaneously estimate serial dependence and structural breaks in non-Gaussian settings without relying on Gaussian approximations.
3.  **Real-Time Parameter Learning:** In surveillance contexts with small initial samples, the transition from fixed "Phase I" estimates to "Phase II" monitoring is often abrupt. Future research is needed on **cautious learning schemes** that update parameters in real-time as more data becomes available while still maintaining strict in-control performance guarantees.

In this paper, we **aim to extend the two-stage changepoint–copula framework to incorporate covariate-conditional dependencies and AR(1)-driven latent processes**, allowing for a more rigorous attribution of identified structural shifts to large-scale physical drivers while explicitly accounting for serial memory in non-stationary count time series.
