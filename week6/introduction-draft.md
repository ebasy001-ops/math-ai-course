# Introduction

Understanding and detecting structural change in environmental and public‑health time series is essential for both scientific insight and operational decision‑making. Changes in mean, trend, or dependence structure can signal regime shifts in climate (e.g., storm frequency or teleconnections) or emerging outbreaks in sparse surveillance data; yet standard methods often misrepresent uncertainty when data are limited or non‑stationary. This introduction synthesizes recent methodological developments and remaining challenges drawing on the project sources (e.g., Lund & Shi, 2025; Hossain & Diawara, 2026; Heidema et al., 2026; Morton et al., 2001; Behrens et al., 2004) and the NotebookLM synthesis of the library.

Research in this area splits naturally into three interlinked themes. First, changepoint detection and structural‑change procedures aim to identify shifts in mean, slope, or variance that mark regime changes in climatological series or counts (Lund & Shi, 2025). Modern approaches include penalized‑likelihood and multivariate extensions that can detect simultaneous breaks across multiple series, and joinpoint or two‑phase regressions for slope changes (Lund & Shi, 2025; Hossain & Diawara, 2026). Second, monitoring and statistical process control (SPC) methods focus on real‑time detection of departures from an in‑control state—particularly when counts are small. Poisson CUSUM, Shewhart, and EWMA charts have been adapted for surveillance, and recent work develops exact in‑control guarantees to address parameter estimation uncertainty (Morton et al., 2001; Heidema et al., 2026). Third, dependence and extreme‑value modeling addresses time‑varying associations and rare events: copula‑based frameworks and EVT with explicit threshold uncertainty provide tools to model non‑stationary teleconnections and tail risk (Hossain & Diawara, 2026; Behrens et al., 2004).

Despite progress, several methodological gaps persist and motivate the present research. First, autocorrelation and latent temporal dependence can inflate false changepoint detections; methods that explicitly model AR(1)‑like structure within changepoint tests are needed to maintain correct size and power. Second, pairwise copula fits lack coherence in high dimensions; vine or fully multivariate constructions are necessary to represent complex cross‑series dependence consistently. Third, attribution requires covariate‑conditional models: embedding climate indices (ENSO, IOD) into dependence parameters would move analysis from detection to physical attribution. Fourth, surveillance practice needs online parameter‑learning schemes that adapt thresholds safely as data accrue while preserving guaranteed in‑control error rates (Heidema et al., 2026). Finally, extreme‑value analysis must better account for threshold uncertainty and model the bulk below thresholds (e.g., mixture or Student‑t center models) to improve tail inferences (Behrens et al., 2004).

This paper develops a practical pipeline that combines robust changepoint procedures, coherent dependence modeling, and cautious online updating to address these gaps. Drawing on the two‑stage changepoint–copula strategy and exact SPC adjustments in the library, we (1) present an AR‑aware changepoint test designed for count series, (2) demonstrate a vine‑copula workflow for coherent multivariate dependence across basins, and (3) propose a cautious online update scheme for monitoring thresholds with maintained in‑control guarantees. We validate the approach with tropical cyclone counts and a surveillance case study, showing improved calibration and interpretable attribution.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work and the theoretical foundations; Section 3 presents the methodology; Section 4 contains simulations and data applications; Section 5 discusses limitations and next steps.
